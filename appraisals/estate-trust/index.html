Estate & Trust Appraisals in San Diego

Independent valuations of San Diego homes held in an estate or living trust, prepared for trustees, heirs and advisors who must divide, distribute, sell or account for real property.

Settling a trust or an estate means turning a list of assets into clear decisions, and the house is often the largest item on that list. Brian Ward appraises single family homes, condominiums and two to four unit buildings for trustees, executors and the professionals who advise them. The report can carry a current effective date for a distribution or buyout, or a past date when the trust document or a tax filing calls for one. Because the work is not tied to any loan, the analysis answers the questions trust administration actually raises, such as how to split equity fairly, whether to sell or keep, and what figure to report to beneficiaries.

What a trust appraisal settles

A trustee owes beneficiaries an honest accounting, and a credible value is the starting point for nearly every decision that follows. The appraisal can support an equal division when one heir keeps the home and pays the others, document the value at the moment a subtrust is funded, or show beneficiaries why a particular listing price was chosen. It also gives the trustee a written record made by a neutral party, which can matter if anyone later questions whether the property was handled prudently.

Choosing the effective date

Trust work frequently involves more than one date. The settlor's death may set the tax basis, while a distribution months later calls for a current figure. Some trusts split into survivor and bypass shares, and the funding date of each share can become important. Brian Ward confirms with the trustee or the attorney which date or dates the assignment needs before the work begins, and each value in the report is labeled clearly so an accountant or beneficiary can tell them apart.

From first call to delivered report

The trustee or the attorney sends the address, the relevant dates and a short note about the intended use. Brian Ward replies with a fee and a timeline. If an inspection is part of the scope, a visit is arranged through whoever holds the keys, and it usually takes under an hour for a single family home. The research, comparable selection and reconciliation follow, and the report is delivered in PDF form, ready to share with co-trustees, beneficiaries and counsel.

Where trust assignments get complicated

Family homes held for decades sometimes have additions that were never permitted, a granny flat that a relative still occupies, or a roof and plumbing that have not been touched in a generation. Multiple trustees may disagree about whether to sell, and beneficiaries living out of state may never see the house in person. A rental building may have long-term tenants paying below market rent. Each of these affects value, and the report explains how it was weighed so that the people who were not in the room can follow the logic.

In the City of San Diego

Trust homes across the City of San Diego range from Kensington Spanish revivals and Mission Hills Craftsmen to Tierrasanta and Mira Mesa tract houses and Mission Beach cottages. Older properties in historic districts or on Mills Act contracts carry restrictions that a buyer inherits, and the report notes them when they apply. Coastal neighborhoods such as Ocean Beach and La Jolla fall inside the Coastal Overlay Zone, where permitting for additions is slower and can shape what a buyer is willing to pay. Many San Diego trusts also hold a duplex or small multifamily building in North Park, City Heights or Normal Heights, which brings rent data into the analysis.

What to have ready

  • A copy of the trust or letters of administration showing who can order the report
  • The date of death and any later distribution or funding dates
  • Lease agreements and rent rolls for any rental units
  • Permits or plans for additions or remodels
  • A list of known repair issues
  • Names of co-trustees or counsel who should receive the report

Questions about estate & trust appraisals

Can one report cover both the date of death and today's value?

Yes. When a trustee needs a basis figure and a current figure for distribution, Brian Ward can include both effective dates in the assignment. Each value is supported by its own set of comparable sales and is labeled so the accountant and beneficiaries can tell which number serves which purpose. The quote reflects the added research.

Do the beneficiaries need to approve the appraiser?

That depends on the trust document and on the relationship among the beneficiaries. Many trustees simply order the report and share it. When tension exists, some families agree on the appraiser in advance so nobody later claims the number favored one side. Brian Ward works only for the stated client and does not advocate for any beneficiary.

Will the appraisal reflect needed repairs?

Yes. The value reflects the property as it actually exists on the effective date, including worn finishes, dated systems or visible damage. If the trustee is weighing a sale as is versus repairing first, the report can describe the condition clearly enough for the trustee to discuss that choice with an agent or contractor.
