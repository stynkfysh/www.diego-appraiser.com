Divorce Appraisals in San Diego

Neutral, court-ready valuations of a San Diego marital home or rental property, prepared for spouses, attorneys and mediators working out a property division or buyout.

In a divorce, the family home is often both the largest asset and the most emotional one, and the two people involved rarely see its value the same way. Brian Ward provides independent appraisals that give both sides a supported number to negotiate from. Reports can be ordered jointly, by one spouse through counsel, or at the request of a mediator, and they can be written as of a current date or a past one such as the date of separation. The goal is a figure that holds up under questioning, written in plain terms that a judge, a mediator or either party can follow without needing an appraiser to translate.

What the report establishes

The appraisal produces a market value for the residence, or for a rental property the couple owns, along with the evidence behind it. That value feeds directly into the equalization calculation when one spouse keeps the property and the other receives an offsetting share. It can also clarify how much equity exists after the mortgage, so that both sides understand what a buyout would actually cost. A clear, well-supported value often shortens negotiations because the argument shifts from guessing to testing the evidence.

Valuation dates in family law

California Family Code section 2552 generally calls for community assets to be valued as near as practicable to the time of trial, though the court can choose a different date for good cause. Separate property claims, reimbursement issues and Moore Marsden calculations can bring older dates into play, such as the date of marriage or the date a separate-property home was refinanced. Brian Ward confirms the needed dates with counsel before beginning, and a single report can carry more than one effective date when the case requires it.

Keeping the process even-handed

Both spouses often still have a stake in the home, and sometimes only one is living there. Brian Ward schedules the visit through the client or counsel and keeps the inspection focused on the property itself. Neither party is told what the other said, and the appraiser does not offer opinions about who should keep the house. The report describes what was observed and how the market evidence was weighed, so either attorney can probe the reasoning without suspecting a hidden thumb on the scale.

Common sticking points

Disputes often center on improvements one spouse paid for, such as a kitchen remodel or a new pool, and whether they added as much value as they cost. Others involve a home that has slipped into disrepair since the separation, or a rental unit whose rent is below market. When a case proceeds to trial, Brian Ward can explain the report in a deposition or on the stand, and the earlier conversation about scope helps ensure the report already addresses the questions opposing counsel is likely to raise.

In the City of San Diego

Family law matters for the City of San Diego are generally heard at the Central Courthouse at 1100 Union Street downtown, and counsel there routinely see appraisals challenged on comparable selection. Values can swing sharply between nearby pockets, such as a canyon-rim house in University Heights versus an interior lot a few streets away, or a Rancho Bernardo home on the golf course versus one backing to Interstate 15. Accessory dwelling units built under the city's ADU rules are another frequent topic, since they can add rental income but are not always valued dollar for dollar. Condo divorces downtown or in Hillcrest add HOA dues, special assessments and parking rights to the discussion.

Who orders this report

  • Spouses negotiating a buyout
  • Family law attorneys
  • Mediators and collaborative divorce teams
  • Certified divorce financial analysts
  • Parties representing themselves
  • Court-appointed neutrals

What to have ready

  • The case number and the names of both attorneys, if any
  • The valuation date or dates counsel has requested
  • Access arrangements and the name of the occupying spouse
  • A list of improvements made during the marriage with approximate dates
  • Mortgage statements if equity needs to be discussed
  • Leases for any rented units or accessory dwelling

Questions about divorce appraisals

Should we each hire our own appraiser?

That is a strategy decision for you and your attorneys. Many couples save money by agreeing on one neutral appraiser and sharing the report. When one side expects a dispute, each may order its own, and the two reports are compared. Brian Ward accepts either kind of engagement but always works for a single named client and stays impartial in the analysis.

Can you value the home as of our date of separation?

Yes. A retrospective appraisal can be written as of the separation date or any other past date counsel identifies. Sales from around that time are used, and condition is reconstructed from photos and records. Whether that date is legally relevant to your case is a question for your attorney, not the appraiser.

Will you testify if the case goes to trial?

Yes. Brian Ward can be deposed or testify about the report he prepared. Testimony and preparation time are billed separately from the appraisal itself, and the rate is provided in advance. Letting the appraiser know early that trial is possible helps shape the scope so the report anticipates likely questions.
