Date of Death Appraisals in San Diego

A retrospective appraisal that pins down what a San Diego house was worth on the date its owner died, for stepped-up basis, estate filings and fair division among heirs.

When someone passes away owning a house, a condo or a small rental building, the value that matters is usually the one that existed on the date of death, not today's number. Brian Ward prepares retrospective appraisals that reach back to that specific day, using sales that closed around it and the condition the property was in at the time. Executors, trustees and their CPAs rely on these reports to set the new cost basis, to support estate filings and to give siblings a neutral figure before anyone talks about buying out a share. The work is non-lending, so the report is written for the family and its advisors rather than for a bank.

Why the date of death figure carries so much weight

Under federal tax rules, inherited real estate generally receives a new basis equal to its market value when the owner died. That single number can decide how much gain, if any, the heirs report when the property later sells. If the figure is too low, the family may pay tax on appreciation that happened before they ever owned it. If it is unsupported, an auditor can question it years later, long after the comparable sales have faded from memory. A credentialed appraisal written at the time, with the reasoning shown, gives the CPA something defensible to attach to the file.

Looking backward without hindsight

A retrospective assignment asks what a reasonable buyer would have paid on a past date, using only information that existed then. Brian Ward selects sales that closed in the months surrounding the death, and listings that were active at that moment, and avoids leaning on later sales that reflect a different market. Condition is reconstructed from photos, the family's recollections and any inspection or listing records, because a home that has since been emptied, cleaned or repaired may look quite different today. The report states the effective date plainly so no reader confuses it with a current value.

How an assignment typically unfolds

It starts with a short call or email to confirm the address, the date of death, the intended users and whether a visit is useful. Brian Ward then quotes a fee and a delivery date before anything is scheduled. If the home is still furnished or unchanged, an inspection can record its features directly; if it has been sold or renovated, a desktop format built on records and photos may be the better fit. Research, analysis and writing follow, and the finished report arrives as a PDF that can be forwarded to the attorney, CPA or trust company.

Situations that need extra care

Some deaths happened years before anyone orders a report, so the market evidence must be pulled from an older period and condition is harder to document. Others involve a partial interest, such as a decedent who owned half of a duplex with a sibling, where the intended use determines whether the whole property or only a fractional share is valued. Unpermitted additions, deferred maintenance and a tenant in place can each move the number. A second date sometimes matters too, such as an alternate valuation date six months later, and the report can address both when asked.

In the City of San Diego

Inherited homes in the City of San Diego often come with decades of history. A Clairemont tract house bought in the 1960s, a Point Loma ranch with a bay view or a North Park bungalow on a Mills Act contract can each carry a property tax bill far below market. Under Proposition 19, a child who inherits generally keeps that low base only if the home becomes the child's principal residence within a year and stays within the value limit, so families often need the figure for the Assessor as well as for basis. In formal probate, San Diego Superior Court relies on a probate referee for the inventory, yet many executors still want an independent report for tax purposes.

Who orders this report

  • Executors and personal representatives
  • Successor trustees of a living trust
  • Heirs planning to sell an inherited home
  • CPAs and enrolled agents preparing estate or income tax returns
  • Estate planning and probate attorneys
  • Family members weighing a buyout of a sibling's share

What to have ready

  • The exact date of death
  • A copy of the death certificate or trust paperwork naming the decision maker
  • Any photos showing the home's condition around that date
  • Notes on upgrades or repairs made before and after the death
  • Contact details for whoever can provide access, if a visit is planned
  • The name of the CPA or attorney who will read the report

Questions about date of death appraisals

How far back can a date of death appraisal go?

There is no fixed limit, but the further back the date, the thinner the evidence becomes. Brian Ward has completed retrospective reports for deaths that occurred several years earlier, relying on recorded sales, archived listings and whatever photos the family can find. Older dates may take more research time, which is reflected in the quote before the work begins.

The house has already been cleaned out and listed. Is it too late?

Usually not. The appraisal values the home in the condition it was in when the owner died, so photos, prior listing records and the family's description of its condition fill in what can no longer be seen. If the home has been remodeled since, the report explains which features were considered and which were set aside as later changes.

Does the probate referee's value replace this appraisal?

Not always. In a formal California probate, the referee appraises real estate for the court's inventory, and that figure is often used. Many families and CPAs still want an independent, fully documented report for basis, for trust administration outside probate or for a dispute among heirs. Your attorney can tell you which figure the case needs.
