Skyline home appraisals

Postwar hillside tracts above Paradise Valley

Skyline is a hilly southeastern San Diego neighborhood of postwar tract homes, with Morse High School and open space along the Jamacha canyon system. Slope, views and lot usability vary noticeably across its curving streets.

The Skyline market

Skyline lies north of Paradise Valley Road, with Encanto to the west, Lomita to the east, Jamacha to the north and Bay Terraces to the south. It is part of the Skyline-Paradise Hills community planning area. The neighborhood is often called Skyline Hills, and its streets wind over hills cut by canyons that feed Chollas Creek and Paradise Creek. Morse High School is the area's largest school, and the Skyline Hills Library serves the community. Housing is mostly single-family homes from the 1950s and 1960s, with a strong Filipino-American community dating to Navy families who settled here. Brian Ward considers slope, lot usability, views and additions carefully, because those features separate otherwise similar tract homes.

How Skyline took shape

Skyline developed mainly in the 1950s and 1960s as postwar tract housing spread east of established neighborhoods. Restrictive covenants and discriminatory practices initially limited who could buy homes here. As those barriers fell in the 1960s and 1970s, the neighborhood became more diverse. From the Vietnam era through the 1990s, many Filipino Navy families settled in Skyline, drawn by affordable homes near Naval Base San Diego. The community has since remained a center of Filipino-American and Black family life in San Diego.

Homes and housing stock

Most homes are single-story and some two-story tract houses from the 1950s and 1960s, typically between 1,100 and 1,800 square feet, on moderate lots that follow the hills. Many have been expanded or updated over the decades, and some have added units, enclosed patios or converted garages. Canyon-edge lots may offer views or privacy but often have steep rear slopes. Condominiums and apartments are limited. Homes on higher streets may have distant views of the bay or the mountains to the east. Security features such as window bars and wrought-iron gates are common and are noted during inspection.

What moves values in Skyline

Slope and lot usability

Steep lots limit usable area and may reduce value compared with flat lots.

Views from higher streets

Distant views can add value, though usually less than coastal views.

Additions and permits

Many homes have added living area. Permit status affects how much value additions contribute.

Canyon frontage

Canyon-edge lots may gain privacy but face slope and fire considerations.

Street layout

Homes on busier streets face more traffic than cul-de-sacs or interior streets.

Appraisal assignments here

Navy retirees and Filipino-American families who bought here decades ago now pass homes to children, so estate, trust and parent-to-child transfer appraisals are common. Divorce appraisals often involve homes enlarged with additions of uncertain permit status. Immigration matters occasionally call for a documented value of a family's home. Pre-sale and bankruptcy appraisals also arise, and each depends on accurate measurement and permit research for the property.

Skyline appraisal questions

How are additions valued in Skyline homes?

Brian Ward checks permit records for additions and measures the home during inspection. Permitted additions generally add value based on how the market treats comparable square footage, while unpermitted additions receive limited credit because a buyer would bear the cost and risk of legalizing them. The report explains the treatment.

Do views add value in Skyline?

Distant bay or mountain views can add value, though usually less than coastal views do. Brian Ward compares the property with sales that have similar views where possible and supports any adjustment with local data, rather than applying a premium borrowed from other parts of the city.
