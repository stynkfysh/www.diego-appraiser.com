Mission Hills home appraisals

Historic hillside homes above the bay and Old Town

Mission Hills sits on the bluffs between Hillcrest, Old Town and Mission Valley, with many early twentieth-century homes on canyon-cut streets. Views, historic designation and lot slope can change value sharply from one house to the next, which makes careful comparable selection essential.

The Mission Hills market

Mission Hills is mostly detached homes, which is unusual this close to downtown. The streets follow the hills, so blocks change direction around canyons, and houses on the western and southern slopes look over the airport, the bay and Point Loma. The small business district on Washington Street and Goldfinch Street serves the neighborhood, and Presidio Park borders the northwest corner. Several areas are designated historic districts, and many homes have Mills Act contracts. Homes range from modest bungalows to large Spanish Revival and Craftsman houses on canyon lots. For an appraiser, the main issues are view quality, airport noise on the western slopes, historic status, and the steep lots that limit additions. Sales within Mission Hills are usually the best evidence, but the number of truly similar sales in any year can be small.

How Mission Hills took shape

Mission Hills was subdivided starting in the early 1900s, and streetcar service on Washington Street brought buyers up from downtown. Kate Sessions ran a nursery here and planted many of the trees that still line the streets. Architects including Irving Gill, William Templeton Johnson and Richard Requa designed homes in the 1910s and 1920s. Building continued into the 1930s, and the neighborhood was mostly built out by the end of the 1940s. Residents later pushed for historic district designations to protect the older blocks.

Homes and housing stock

Most homes date from 1910 to 1940, in Craftsman, Prairie, Mission Revival, Spanish Colonial Revival, Tudor and Colonial Revival styles. Many lots are irregular because they follow canyon edges, and some houses have basements or lower levels built into the slope. Homes range from small bungalows to houses over 4,000 square feet with view decks and detached garages. Postwar and modern homes are mixed in, along with some small apartment buildings and condominiums near Washington Street and in the lower areas toward Middletown. Many homes have added second units or converted garages, which require a check of permits.

What moves values in Mission Hills

Bay and airport views

Views of the bay, Point Loma, downtown or Mission Valley can add a large premium. The appraisal grades each view by width and permanence, including whether vegetation or new building could block it.

Airport noise

The western and southern slopes are close to San Diego International's flight path and departure noise. Sales with similar exposure are used to measure any discount.

Historic districts and Mills Act

Designated historic districts limit exterior changes, and Mills Act contracts lower property taxes. Both are identified in the report because they affect what buyers can do with a property and what it costs to own.

Steep canyon lots

Slope can limit additions, ADUs and parking, and some lots need retaining walls or geotechnical work. Usable area and access matter more than recorded lot size.

Architect-designed homes

Homes by known architects can sell for more than similar homes by unknown builders. The appraisal looks for comparable sales of significant homes rather than averaging them with ordinary sales.

Appraisal assignments here

Long ownership in Mission Hills makes date-of-death and trust appraisals common, often for homes held by the same family for decades with deferred maintenance or original features. Divorce valuations of view homes need careful view analysis. Pre-sale appraisals help owners price historic homes with few direct comparables. Mills Act contracts and historic designation often come up in property tax and estate-planning work.

Mission Hills appraisal questions

How much is a view worth in Mission Hills?

There is no set percentage. Brian Ward compares sales of homes with similar views and similar homes without views, and grades each view by what is visible, how wide it is, and whether it is likely to last. A wide bay view from the main living area is treated very differently from a partial view from an upstairs window.

Does living in a historic district raise or lower value?

It can do either. Historic districts protect the character of the neighborhood, which buyers often pay for, but they also limit exterior changes and additions. The appraisal compares sales inside and outside the districts when there is enough data, and notes any Mills Act contract that affects property taxes.
