Bankers Hill home appraisals

Victorian mansions and canyon bridges above Balboa Park

Bankers Hill climbs from Little Italy to the western edge of Balboa Park, with turn-of-the-century houses, mid-century apartments and newer condominiums sharing the same blocks. Park frontage, canyon edges and the flight path into Lindbergh Field can all affect the value of one lot compared with the next.

The Bankers Hill market

Bankers Hill sits between downtown and Hillcrest on a ridge cut by Maple Canyon and several smaller canyons. Sixth Avenue runs along Balboa Park, and condominiums there sell partly for the park views. The interior streets have Victorian and Craftsman houses, many divided into apartments, alongside homes designed by architect Irving Gill and others. Fourth and Fifth avenues carry most of the traffic and restaurants. For an appraiser, the mix of housing types is the main challenge. Single-family homes are scarce, many older buildings were converted to multiple units, and condo buildings range from small 1960s complexes to towers with views of the bay and downtown. Aircraft approaching San Diego International pass directly overhead, and how buyers treat that noise depends on the floor and the building.

How Bankers Hill took shape

Wealthy San Diegans, many of them bankers and merchants, built homes on this ridge overlooking the bay in the 1880s and 1890s, which gave the neighborhood its name. Kate Sessions' work in the new City Park, later Balboa Park, and the 1915 Panama-California Exposition made the park-side blocks more desirable. The Quince Street and Spruce Street bridges were built across canyons in the early 1900s, and the First Avenue Bridge followed in 1931. Apartment construction after World War II replaced some mansions, and later condominium projects increased density.

Homes and housing stock

The housing includes Victorian and Queen Anne houses, Craftsman homes, early modern homes by Irving Gill and his contemporaries, and Spanish Revival apartment courts from the 1920s. Many large houses were converted into two to six units over the decades, and some later became condominiums. Sixth Avenue and parts of Fourth and Fifth have 1960s and 1970s mid-rise apartments and condos, plus newer towers. Single-family homes are mostly on canyon-rim streets west of Fourth Avenue, often on narrow or sloped lots. Unit sizes vary from small studios in converted houses to large condos with park or bay views.

What moves values in Bankers Hill

Park and bay views

Units facing Balboa Park, downtown or the bay sell for more than units facing interior courtyards, and the gain depends on floor and how open the view is. Comparables are chosen by view type whenever possible.

Flight path noise

Bankers Hill lies under the final approach to San Diego International. Upper floors and units facing west get the most noise, and that exposure is reflected in paired sales.

Historic designation

Many older homes are designated historic or sit near designated resources, and some have Mills Act contracts that lower property taxes. The appraisal identifies these contracts and any restrictions on alterations.

Converted houses

A house divided into units may have permitted or unpermitted conversions. Permit history affects legal use, income and value, so it is checked before units are counted.

Canyon lots

Lots on Maple Canyon and the smaller canyons can be steep and may limit additions or ADUs, but they often have privacy and views. Usable lot area matters more than the total lot size.

Appraisal assignments here

Trust and estate appraisals are common for long-held Victorian and Craftsman properties, where retrospective values have to account for historic status and deferred maintenance. Partnership buyouts and divorces often involve small multi-unit buildings that were converted decades ago. Property tax appeals come up for Mills Act properties and for condos whose assessed values do not reflect the effect of flight-path noise. Pre-sale appraisals help owners price unusual homes that have few direct comparables.

Bankers Hill appraisal questions

Does a Mills Act contract change the appraised market value?

It does not change market value directly, but it can affect how buyers price a home. The contract runs with the property and lowers property taxes in exchange for preservation obligations. Brian Ward identifies the contract, explains its terms, and considers whether recent sales of Mills Act homes in Uptown show a premium or any discount for the restrictions.

How is a Victorian divided into units valued?

It depends on what the permits allow. If the units are legal, the property is compared with small income properties and may be valued partly on rents. If some units are not permitted, the appraisal reports the legal use and considers the cost and risk of legalizing or removing them, rather than counting every unit.
