North Park home appraisals

Craftsman blocks and busy corridors east of Balboa Park

North Park runs from the eastern edge of Balboa Park to the I-805, centered on University Avenue and 30th Street, with streets of early-1900s bungalows behind the commercial strips. Historic districts, ADUs and new infill under transit-area zoning have widened the price range of similar-looking homes.

The North Park market

North Park is one of the city's largest older neighborhoods, with a dense grid of Craftsman and Spanish Revival homes, bungalow courts and apartment buildings. The main commercial strips on University Avenue, 30th Street and Park Boulevard have restaurants, breweries and the restored Observatory North Park theatre. Morley Field and the eastern side of Balboa Park border the west, and Switzer Canyon and smaller canyons break up the southern and eastern blocks. Several areas are designated historic districts, including Burlingame and the Dryden district. Transit corridors carry frequent buses, and city programs encouraging housing near transit have brought new townhomes and apartment buildings to former single-lot parcels. For an appraiser, North Park requires distinguishing between restored and original homes, historic-district and non-district blocks, and houses with permitted ADUs and those without.

How North Park took shape

Developers subdivided North Park in the early 1900s, after the streetcar extended to University Avenue and 30th Street. Building boomed in the 1910s and 1920s, and the business district at University and 30th grew quickly, marked by the North Park sign. The Depression slowed construction, and postwar suburban growth drew shoppers away by the 1960s. Revival began in the 1990s with restoration of homes and the business district, and the Observatory North Park, built as a movie theatre in 1929, was restored and reopened.

Homes and housing stock

Craftsman bungalows dominate, along with Spanish Revival, Prairie and Mission-style homes from 1910 to 1940, usually on lots of about 5,000 to 7,000 square feet. Many lots have a second unit or a newer ADU. Bungalow courts and small multi-unit buildings are common, and 1960s and 1970s apartment buildings appear on many blocks. Newer townhomes and mid-rise apartments have replaced some older houses near the transit corridors. Homes on canyon rims have larger or steeper lots. Typical houses range from about 900 to 2,000 square feet, often with added rooms that need permit checks.

What moves values in North Park

Historic districts and Mills Act

Burlingame, Dryden and other designated districts limit exterior changes, and Mills Act contracts reduce taxes. Both are noted and considered in comparable selection.

ADUs and second units

A permitted ADU adds rental income and value, while an unpermitted unit carries risk. Permits and unit counts are checked for the subject and for each comparable.

Transit-area infill

Lots near transit corridors may be worth more to developers than to owner-occupants. Zoning and recent land sales are reviewed when a property could be redeveloped.

Restoration quality

A restored Craftsman with original details often sells for more than a remodeled or neglected home of the same size. Condition and quality are adjusted from the inspection.

Corridor and canyon locations

Homes near busy corridors get noise and parking pressure, while canyon-rim homes may have views and privacy. Location adjustments come from paired sales within North Park.

Appraisal assignments here

North Park's long-held bungalows make date-of-death and trust appraisals common, often with original condition and permitted or unpermitted additions to sort out. Divorce valuations often involve homes with ADUs and rental income. Pre-sale appraisals help owners decide whether to sell as a home or as a development site. Mills Act and historic status come up in tax and estate-planning work.

North Park appraisal questions

Does an ADU add dollar-for-dollar value to a North Park home?

Not usually. A permitted ADU adds value, but often less than its construction cost. Brian Ward looks at sales of North Park homes with and without similar ADUs, and at the income those units produce, to measure what buyers actually pay for the extra unit.

Is a North Park home in a historic district worth more?

Often, but not always. Historic districts protect the character of a block, and many buyers want that, while limits on alterations and additions can reduce flexibility. The appraisal compares district and non-district sales and notes any Mills Act contract and its effect on property taxes.
